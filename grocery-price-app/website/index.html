A grocery app where shoppers photograph their receipts, and anyone can ask where is my shopping list cheapest today — and how healthy is what’s in it? The shoppers get the answer free. The by-product is a verified, geo-stamped, item-level shelf-price panel that CPG brands currently pay $2–$12 per store visit to obtain.

01

Why every previous attempt at this died

Consumer grocery price comparison has a graveyard, and it is worth naming the headstones before proposing another one.

mySupermarket raised at least $23.4M from WPP, Greylock and Pitango and closed in March 2020 after fourteen years, unable to make the model sustainable. Basket raised $19.6M on a crowdsourced, Waze-for-groceries thesis and never escaped thin coverage outside dense metros. Trolley.co.uk survived, but received a cease-and-desist over the retailer data its comparisons depend on.

Three structural failures, every time: cold-start coverage (a list optimiser that can price 60% of a basket gives a confidently wrong answer), weak consumer monetisation (a weekly-use utility cannot fund itself on subscriptions), and retailer dependency (a business one legal letter away from having no data).

This plan is three answers to those three failures. If any answer fails, this becomes another headstone — which is why Section 09 sets dated kill criteria rather than milestones.

02

Three mechanics that change the odds

Capture

Receipts, not shelf tags

A shelf-label photo yields one price point and requires altruism. A receipt yields 20–40 price points, is the price actually paid rather than the tag, carries store and timestamp in print, and the shopper scans it for their own reason — spend tracking. Fetch processes 11M receipts a day on exactly that asymmetry, and licenses the result for 35% of its revenue.

Coverage

Chains price by zone, not by store

The standard objection is that a metro needs 3.2M fresh price points. It doesn’t: supermarkets price per chain per regional zone, so 400 stores collapse to 12–18 price groups. Restrict the catalogue to the ~1,500 SKUs carrying 70% of household spend and the cold start drops by 25–40×. The arithmetic is on the till roll below.

Cross

Price × health is empty, and hard to enter

Yuka has 80M+ users scoring health and no price capability — and cannot build one, because its moat is a public refusal of all brand money. The price apps have no nutrition model and monetise through exactly the brand relationships that poison a health score. Neither side can cross. We start on both.

03

What the shopper actually gets

Each step in the loop pays the user before any network effect exists. That is the difference between a product and a data-collection scheme.

SCAN → UNDERSTAND → DECIDE → CONTRIBUTE
 
Photograph the receipt. “€412 this month. Your personal inflation: +6.2%.
  You overpaid €11.40 against the cheapest nearby store.”
 
Open your list. “18 items. Lidl €47.10 · Auchan €54.80 · coverage 94%.
  3 items are healthier for the same money.”
 
Your scan refreshes the zone panel for everyone — anonymised, aggregated, never sold per person.

The features that make it a business, not a utility

  • Personal inflation rate. Your CPI on your basket. Works from your receipts alone, at zero network density — the strongest possible day-one retention hook, and screenshot-shareable.
  • “You overpaid €X” verdict after every shop. Turns a passive tracker into a habit with a reward signal.
  • Cheaper and healthier substitution. The unoccupied cross, expressed as one button.
  • Shrinkflation detector. Unit price over pack size across time — impossible without a longitudinal panel, and irresistible to journalists.
  • Two-store split with a time cost. “Splitting saves €9.40 and costs 20 minutes.” Households think this way; nobody models it.
  • A published monthly city basket index. The PR engine and the B2B sales collateral are the same artefact.
  • Confidence-scored prices that visibly decay. Honesty about staleness is what stops trust collapse when a price is wrong.

The bright line. Money never changes a ranking. Sponsored placements sit outside the price and health ordering, clearly labelled, under a published policy — and the B2B business carries a separate name so the consumer app’s independence is structural, not promised.

Why 2026 and not 2019

The enabling shift is technical and recent: vision-model receipt extraction turned messy multilingual thermal paper from a research problem into a ~€0.004 API call in about twenty-four months. Around it, 74% of consumers report noticing rising prices, 41% have shifted to discounters, the median shopper abandons a loyal brand at a 16% price rise, off-site retail media is growing 29.5% a year while 89% of incremental spend goes to two platforms, and Open Food Facts has crossed 3M products — making the health layer free infrastructure instead of a multi-year data project.

Geography (assumption, testable). This plan assumes a CEE metro launch — Warsaw or Kyiv — on chain concentration, price sensitivity, engineering cost and the underrated advantage of mandatory fiscal receipts, which are standardised and machine-friendly. Launch one metro, not one country: thin national coverage is precisely how Basket stalled.

09

Verdict, and when to stop

Yes, this is a business — but not the business it first looks like.

As a consumer price-comparison app, the expected outcome is a well-loved product that cannot pay for itself, and the category has a clear track record of exactly that. As a consumer-funded data acquisition machine for a B2B price panel, with a health cross no incumbent can copy, the economics work: 86% gross margin, LTV/CAC of 5–9×, breakeven inside three years on under €600k of cumulative burn.

The realistic band is a €3–10M regional data-and-consumer business within four to five years, with credible acquirers in retail intelligence (NielsenIQ, Circana, Profitero), receipts (Fetch), leaflets (Blix, Bonial), or a health app that needs price. It is not venture-scale in one country — multi-country replication is what changes that, and the cost of building each market’s catalogue is the variable that decides whether replication is cheap. Test that in year two, not year four.

Kill criteria — decide honestly against these

  • Month 06Core-basket coverage below 70%, or fewer than 200 weekly contributors → the crowdsourcing model does not work. Stop, or pivot to scraping plus B2B only.
  • Month 09Week-4 retention below 20% → the spend-tracker hook is not habit-forming, and the whole loop fails with it.
  • Month 14Zero paying ShelfSignal contracts → the B2B thesis is wrong and consumer revenue cannot carry the company. Wind down or sell the data asset.
  • Month 18Blended CAC above €4 with organic share under 50% → the organic path is closed and the economics do not survive paid acquisition.

First 90 days — cheapest possible de-risking

  1. Score three candidate metros against the geography framework and pick one.
  2. Collect 500 real receipts there and measure actual extraction accuracy and cost per receipt. This single test de-risks more of the plan than anything else you can do for the money.
  3. Measure real price dispersion on 200 core SKUs across the metro’s chains. Under ~10% dispersion, the consumer proposition is too weak and the plan should be reconsidered regardless of everything else on this page.
  4. Test the zone hypothesis empirically: do stores of one chain in one city actually price identically? Quantify it.
  5. Ship the receipt-scan and spend-tracker loop to 100 users; instrument scans per user per week.
  6. Take the dispersion finding to five CPG brands as a ShelfSignal pilot conversation — before the app is finished.
